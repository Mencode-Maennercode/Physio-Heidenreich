What I do at your home

The full range of physiotherapy in a home visit. My focus is neurology — the other treatments belong just as much.

Treatments

What I treat

Complete and without compromise — a home visit is not a reduced programme.

  • Neurological treatment

    Walking, balance, fine motor skills and safety in daily life — more on this in the section below.

    After a stroke, with Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis or polyneuropathy.

  • Exercise therapy

    Active and passive exercise treatment for strength, mobility and coordination.

    For complaints in joints, spine and muscles.

  • Mobilisation of joints and muscles

    Hands-on treatment: making joints move more freely, stretching shortened muscles, loosening tissue.

    For restricted movement, stiffness and muscular tension.

  • Post-operative care

    Continuing treatment after hospital and rehabilitation, matched to the loading limits you were given.

    After joint replacement, spinal and soft tissue surgery.

  • Fall prevention and gait training

    Balance, standing up safely, handling walking aids — and a look at your home.

    For unsteadiness, dizziness and after falls.

  • Manual lymphatic drainage

    Gentle technique to stimulate lymph flow, with bandaging where needed.

    For swelling after surgery and with lymphoedema.

  • Support with long-term conditions

    Longer-term care with an exercise programme for in between.

    For conditions that are not finished after six appointments.

  • Instructing family members

    Positioning, transfers, helping someone walk — the handling that makes daily life safer.

    For everyone caring at home who wants to protect their own back.

Why this works better at home

Neurological therapy aims at daily life. So daily life is where you practise.

01

You practise on your own stairs

Not on stairs in an exercise room. What you want back are particular movements in particular places: your own bathroom, your own bed, the way to your front door.

02

Nothing breaks on the way home

“It worked in the practice, but not at home” is the most common problem in neurological therapy. If therapy happens at home, that gap does not exist.

03

The surroundings can be changed straight away

Trip hazards, seat heights, grab rails, walking routes. I see most of it in ten minutes — and we can change it together instead of talking about it.

04

Family members are there

Whoever helps every day learns the handling where it is needed. That takes real weight off and makes a lot possible between appointments.

05

No energy spent on the journey

After a stroke or with multiple sclerosis, half the day's energy otherwise goes on getting there and back. Here that energy goes into the treatment.

06

Familiar surroundings, the same face

With problems of orientation or memory this is often what makes therapy work at all. You always have the same therapist — no changing practitioners.

One day, five places

Where a treatment at home actually happens

Not in a treatment room, but along the route you walk every day anyway.

  1. 01 · At the bed

    The first transfer of the day

    Turning over, getting to the edge of the bed, standing up. If that works safely, it carries the whole day. If it does not, little else helps.

  2. 02 · In the bathroom

    The tightest space in the home

    Moving across a few square metres, finding support without pulling up on the basin. Five minutes here show me which grab rail is missing.

  3. 03 · In the hallway

    Walking, turning, thresholds

    Turning around in a narrow space, not catching on a door threshold, getting through a normal door with a walker or stick. Whether walking is safe shows here — not on ten metres of open floor.

  4. 04 · On the stairs

    Up and down, in the right order

    Up with the stronger leg, down with the affected one. Practised on your handrail, at your step height, in your light.

  5. 05 · At the front door

    What most people are aiming for

    Getting outside alone again. The threshold, the key, the first step out — and the way back, which is often the harder one.
